# Recurrent Action Transformer with Memory

Abstract 

Transformers have become increasingly popular in offline reinforcement learning 1

(RL) due to their ability to treat agent trajectories as sequences, reframing policy 2

learning as a sequence modeling task. However, in partially observable environ-3

ments (POMDPs), effective decision-making depends on retaining information 4

about past events – something that standard transformers struggle with due to 5

the quadratic complexity of self-attention, which limits their context length. One 6

solution to this problem is to extend transformers with memory mechanisms. We 7

propose the Recurrent Action Transformer with Memory (RATE) , a novel 8

transformer-based architecture for offline RL that incorporates a recurrent memory 9

mechanism designed to regulate information retention. We evaluate RATE across a 10 

diverse set of environments: memory-intensive tasks (ViZDoom-Two-Colors, T-11 

Maze, Memory Maze, Minigrid-Memory, and POPGym), as well as standard Atari 12 

and MuJoCo benchmarks. Our comprehensive experiments demonstrate that RATE 13 

significantly improves performance in memory-dependent settings while remaining 14 

competitive on standard tasks across a broad range of baselines. These findings 15 

underscore the pivotal role of integrated memory mechanisms in offline RL and es-16 

tablish RATE as a unified, high-capacity architecture for effective decision-making 17 

over extended horizons. 18 

1 Introduction 19 

Originally developed for Natural Language Processing (NLP), transformers [ 50 ] have recently 20 

demonstrated strong performance across a wide range of Reinforcement Learning (RL) settings [ 1, 30 ]. 21 

They have been successfully applied to online [ 36 , 15 ], offline [ 10 , 23 , 52 ], model-based [ 9 , 42 ], 22 

and in-context RL [ 40 , 17 , 43 ]. In particular, transformers show promise for tackling long-horizon 23 

credit assignment and operating in memory-intensive environments [ 34 , 17 , 15 , 36 ], provided the 24 

full trajectory fits within the model context. Despite their success, transformers face fundamental 25 

limitations when applied to long sequences due to the quadratic complexity of self-attention [ 25 ], 26 

which restricts their applicability in long-horizon inference tasks. While various techniques have 27 

been proposed to extend the context window [ 13 , 7 ], these approaches often suffer from training 28 

instability [ 54 ] or rely on task-specific sparse attention patterns that may not generalize well beyond 29 

NLP [ 6, 53 ]. Memory-augmented transformers offer a promising alternative by enabling access to past 30 

information without expanding the context length. Motivated by advances in memory mechanisms 31 

for NLP models [ 13 , 7], we investigate how such approaches can be adapted to RL. Unlike NLP, 32 

RL involves structured and modality-rich inputs – observations, actions, and rewards – that require 33 

domain-specific encoding, and frequently exhibit high sparsity in both reward signal and observations. 34 

In RL, memory usually refers either to using past information within an episode [ 27 , 34 ], or to 35 

transferring experience across environments [ 24 , 48 ], aiding generalization, sample efficiency, and 36 

Meta-RL [14, 51], and we focus on the former. 37

2 Background 74 

Offline RL. In RL [ 47 ], a task is formalized as a Markov Decision Process (MDP): ⟨S , A, P, R⟩ ,75 

where s ∈ S are states, a ∈ A are actions, P(s′|s, a ) is a transition function, and r = R(s, a ) is a 76 

reward function. States satisfy the Markov property: P(st+1 |st) = P(st+1 |s1, . . . , s t). A trajectory τ77 

of length T is a sequence (s0, a 0, r 0, . . . , s T −1, a T −1, r T −1), where rt = R(st, a t) is the immediate 78 

reward at the timestep t. The return-to-go [ 10 ] Rt = PT −1 

> t′=t

rt′ is the sum of future rewards from 79 

t. The goal is to learn a policy π maximizing the expected return. While online RL iteratively 80 

collects trajectories through environment interaction, offline RL uses a fixed dataset of trajectories, 81 

making it suitable for scenarios where environment interaction is costly or risky. A popular offline RL 82 

method, Decision Transformer (DT) [ 10 ], models return-conditioned trajectories with a GPT-style 83 

architecture, avoiding value estimation. However, its fixed context window limits performance in 84 

tasks with delayed rewards or long-term dependencies, motivating memory-augmented models. 85 

POMDP. In real-world, agents often receive partial observations rather than full states, breaking the 86 

Markov property. For instance, a robot using only camera input or an agent relying on past context. 87 

Such cases are modeled as Partially Observable MDPs (POMDPs): ⟨S , A, O, P, R, Z⟩ , where o ∈ O 88 

are observations and Zas′o = P (ot+1 |st+1 = s′, a t = a) defines the observation function. Since 89 

single observations are insufficient, agents must use history to infer useful state representations. 90

Transformers excel at sequence modeling, including 92 

offline RL [ 10 , 22 ], but struggle with long-horizon 93 

tasks due to fixed context and quadratic attention cost. 94 

In memory tasks, agents must recall information seen 95 

thousands of steps earlier—something models like 96 

DT cannot do once cues fall outside context. We pro-97 

pose the Recurrent Action Transformer with Mem-98 

ory (RATE) , which introduces segment-level recur-99 

rence and dynamic memory control. RATE processes 100 

trajectories in segments, using lightweight memory 101 

and a learnable Memory Retention Valve (MRV) 102 

to decide what to retain or discard. In T-Maze [ 34 ], 103 

the agent receives a one-bit cue o0 at the first step 104 

indicating whether to turn left or right at the end of 105 

a maze. Solving the task requires remembering this 106 

cue despite sparse rewards. DT fails once o0 leaves 107 

the context, making retrieval at inference impossible. 108 

Figure 2 shows this: DT attends to o0 only when it fits 109 

the context, while RATE segments the input and prop-110 

agates the memory embeddings, preserving the cue 111 

to the end and enabling explicit memory retention. 112

Naively forwarding memory embeddings leads to error accumulation or overwriting of relevant 127 

information. To address this, we introduce the Memory Retention Valve (MRV) , a cross-attention 128 

module that filters new memory tokens through the lens of the previous ones (Algorithm 2): 129 

MRV (Mn, M n+1 ) = FFN (MultiHead (Query = Mn, Key = Mn+1 , Value = Mn+1 )) (1) This mechanism allows Mn to control what to retain or overwrite when updating to Mn+1 . Unlike 130 

static recurrence, it preserves sparse, long-range information. RATE overcomes DT’s limits by 131 

3extending context with recurrence, preserving early cues via MRV, and retaining key events in sparse 132 

settings. As a result, RATE solves tasks where DT fails, generalizes beyond training, and remains 133 

competitive on standard MDPs. 134

across segments. These tokens retain access to o0 even in later segments, demonstrating RATE’s 138

tasks from the POPGym benchmark suite [ 32 ], which covers a wide range of partially observable 272 

RL scenarios. The benchmark is split into 31 memory puzzle tasks and 15 reactive POMDP tasks .273

failure to exploit long-term dependencies. Notably, DT scores −3.49 and BC-MLP drops to −11 .91 ,277

On reactive tasks, all models perform better, but the gap between memory-based and non-memory 279 

models narrows. RATE, DT, and BC-LSTM show almost the same results, suggesting that the greatest 280 

performance gains from RATE’s memory mechanisms occur on memory puzzle tasks. For simpler 281 

reactive POMDPs, lightweight memory mechanisms appear sufficient. These results also underscore 282

architecture does not hinder performance in simpler tasks while offering clear benefits in those with 284

6 Related Work 369 

Transformers in RL. Transformers have been applied to online [ 36 , 27 , 33 , 43 , 28 ], offline [ 10 ,370 

22 , 52 ], and model-based RL [ 9]. While prior work often relies on compact observations or known 371 

dynamics [ 29 , 23 ], RATE targets long-horizon credit assignment and memory challenges in partially 372 

observable environments, using DT [ 10 ] as a baseline. A recent extension, Long-Short Decision 373 

Transformer (LSDT) [ 52 ], augments DT with two context windows but still lacks an explicit, learnable 374 

memory. Retrieval-augmented variants, e.g. RA-DT [ 43 ], index external trajectories for in-context 375 

planning, while Fast and Forgetful Memory (FFM) [ 33 ] and Stable Hadamard Memory (SHM) [ 28 ]376 

explore lightweight recurrent memory slots with improved stability. 377 

RNNs in RL. Recurrent models like LSTM [21] and GRU [12] have long been used for memory in 378 

RL. DLSTM [ 45 ] replaces transformers with LSTM to support sequential decision-making. However, 379 

RNNs often struggle with long-term dependencies, especially in sparse-reward settings [34]. 380 

SSMs in RL. SSMs such as S4 [ 19 ] and Mamba [ 18 ] offer efficient alternatives to attention, showing 381 

strong offline RL results [ 3, 35 , 8]. These models rely on linear dynamics and their ability to handle 382 

memory-intensive generalization remains unclear. 383 

Memory-Augmented Transformers. Memory extensions like T ransformer-XL [ 13 ], Compressive 384 

Transformer [ 41 ], and RMT [ 7 ] improve context handling via caching or compression. RATE builds 385 

on these ideas by combining token-level memory, hidden-state caching, and a novel MRV gate. 386 

7 Limitations 387 

While RATE is tailored for long-horizon, memory-intensive tasks, its complexity may be unnecessary 388 

in fully observable or short-term settings where simpler recurrent models suffice. Nonetheless, RATE 389 

matches or exceeds their performance across all tasks. Future work may explore adaptive variants 390 

that scale memory based on task complexity. 391 

8 Conclusion 392 

We propose the Recurrent Action Transformer with Memory (RATE ), a transformer-based 393 

architecture for offline RL that combines attention with recurrence for long-horizon decision-making. 394 

RATE integrates memory embeddings, hidden state caching, and a Memory Retention Valve (MRV )395 

to selectively retain critical information across segments. RATE achieves state-of-the-art results 396 

on memory-intensive tasks such as T-Maze, Minigrid-Memory, ViZDoom-Two-Colors, Memory 397 

Maze, and POPGym, generalizing up to 9600-step sequences and outperforming both recurrent and 398 

transformer baselines. Theoretical analysis shows that MRV guarantees lower-bounded memory 399 

preservation across updates, and ablation studies confirm its importance for long-horizon stability. 400 

Despite its memory focus, RATE also performs competitively on standard benchmarks like Atari and 401 

MuJoCo, demonstrating broad versatility. These results establish RATE as a unified, general-purpose 402 

offline RL model that excels across both short and long temporal contexts. 403

A Discussion: Are RNNs Still Better for Memory? 931 

Our experiments provide a systematic comparison between recurrent and transformer-based archi-932 

tectures in memory-intensive tasks. When trained on short sequences, recurrent models such as 933 

BC-LSTM perform competitively. For example, in the T-Maze environment, BC-LSTM achieves 934 

perfect success rates when trained on sequences up to 150 steps, effectively capturing short-term 935 

dependencies via its internal state dynamics. 936 

However, this advantage quickly fades as training sequences grow longer. Increasing the training 937 

horizon from 150 to 600 steps causes BC-LSTM’s performance to collapse to a 50% success rate 938 

across all inference lengths—even those shorter than the training context—indicating difficulty with 939 

gradient stability and information retention over long spans (Figure 6). In contrast, RATE maintains 940 

consistently high performance under the same conditions, demonstrating stronger scalability with 941 

sequence length. RATE generalizes robustly to inference horizons up to 9600 steps (28,800 tokens), 942 

reflecting the effectiveness of its hybrid memory design. The architecture combines token-based 943 

recurrence with gated memory updates via the Memory Retention Valve (MRV), enabling reliable 944 

propagation of sparse information across long temporal distances. 945 

These findings extend to more complex environments. In ViZDoom-Two-Colors and Memory Maze 946 

(Figure 4, Table 1), RATE significantly outperforms BC-LSTM. In ViZDoom, RATE maintains 947 

balanced performance across red and green cues, whereas BC-LSTM exhibits instability and higher 948 

variance. In Memory Maze, RATE achieves substantially higher returns, benefiting from its capacity 949 

to encode and retrieve spatial-temporal patterns over long episodes. 950 

In conclusion, while RNNs remain effective for short-range temporal dependencies, their performance 951 

degrades in long-horizon, sparse-reward, and generalization-critical settings. RATE bridges this gap 952

To investigate agent’s long-term memory on very long environments (the inference trajectory length 1000

The observation vector is thus defined as o = [ y, clue, f lag, noise ], where y represents the vertical 1012 

coordinate. The reward r is given only at the end of the episode and depends on the correctness of 1013 

the agent’s turn at the junction, being 1 for a correct turn and 0 otherwise. This formulation deviates 1014 

from the traditional Passive T-Maze environment [ 34 ] (different observations and reward functions) 1015 

and presents a more intricate set of conditions for the agent to navigate and learn within the given 1016 

time constraint. 1017 

The dataset consists of 2000 of trajectories for each segment of length 30 (i.e. 6000 trajectories for 1018

To train RATE, DT, RMT, and TrXL on Memory Maze, we used the same approach as for ViZDoom-1143 

Two-Colors environment, but instead of using fixed trajectories starting at t = 0 , we sampled 1144 

consecutive 90-step subsequences from the original 1000-step trajectories. Each subsequence was 1145 

sampled with a stride of 90 steps, resulting in approximately 11 training sequences per original 1146 

trajectory. As in the ViZDoom-Two-Colors case, training for DT was performed with a context length 1147 

of K = 90 and for RATE, RMT, and TrXL with a context length of K = 30 and number of segments 1148 

N = 3 , i.e., effective context length Kef f = N × K = 3 × 30 = 90 .1149 

E.4 Minigrid-Memory 1150 

To train baselines in this environment, we used only mazes of fixed size 41 × 41 , ensuring a consistent 1151 

corridor length during training. For evaluation, models were validated on mazes ranging from 11 × 11 1152 

to 501 × 501 , where corridor lengths vary within each grid, enabling assessment of both interpolation 1153 

and extrapolation capabilities. All training trajectories used an episode timeout of 96 steps, while 1154 

27 validation trajectories across all maze sizes used a longer timeout of 500 steps. As in T-Maze, each 1155 

trajectory began at t = 0 , ensuring the cue was always observed. During training, RATE used a 1156 

context length of K = 30 with N = 3 segments, while other baselines (except RMT and TrXL) used 1157 

K = 90 .1158 

E.5 POPGym Suite 1159 

POPGym [ 32 ] comprises 46 tasks of varying memory complexity, including both memory puzzles 1160 

and reactive POMDPs. Since episode lengths vary widely across tasks – from as short as 12 steps 1161 

to as long as 1000 – we ensured a consistent and fair memory evaluation for RATE by setting the 1162 

context length K = T / 3 and using N = 3 segments for every environment, where T denotes the 1163 

maximum episode length of each task. This uniform configuration allowed RATE to process full 1164 

trajectories with recurrent segmentation, ensuring its memory capacity was equally tested across 1165 

tasks of different lengths and difficulties. 1166 

E.6 Atari and MuJoCo 1167 

When training RATE on Atari games and MuJoCo control tasks, sequences of length T = 90 (Atari) 1168 

and T = 60 (MuJoCo) were sampled randomly from the original trajectories in the dataset. These 1169 

trajectories were then divided into N = 3 segments of length K = 30 (Atari) and K = 20 (MuJoCo), 1170 

forming an effective context of length Kef f = N × K = 90 (60 for MuJoCo). 1171 

For Atari, we used the identical experimental design described in the DT paper [ 10 ]. It is worth 1172 

noting that we presented raw scores for Atari, rather than gamer-normalized scores as described in 1173 

the DT paper. Table 4 shows the results for Atari environments. RATE outperforms DT significantly 1174 

in environments like Breakout and Qbert. We attribute this to the observation that, although these 1175 

environments do not explicitly demand memory, intricate dynamics from the past exert a greater 1176 

influence on agent behavior than in environments such as SeaQuest. Actions executed in the past 1177 

notably alter the present state of the environment in Breakout and Qbert, whereas in SeaQuest, such 1178 

actions hold little significance. For instance, the emergence of enemies and divers in SeaQuest is 1179 

entirely independent of the agent’s prior actions. 1180 

For MuJoCo, our findings suggest that the conventional strategy of utilizing return is not suitable 1181 

for our segment-based scheme. The issue arises during the trajectory, where the agent’s return 1182 

persistently diminishes. However, the true value of the agent’s state at the onset and conclusion of the 1183 

episode could remain unchanged, provided the agent’s policy performs consistently well. To rectify 1184 

this discrepancy, we propose a novel evaluation strategy for MuJoCo tasks. In this approach, each 1185 

segment commences with the maximum return, simulating the scenario where the agent initiates the 1186 

trajectory anew. This method effectively mitigates the aforementioned issue, enhancing the accuracy 1187 

of our evaluation process. Our MuJoCo experiments in Table 3 show that this benefits performance 1188 

significantly for some environments. Thus, using RATE allowed us to obtain the best metrics for 1189 

MuJoCo in 4/9 cases compared to the other baselines. RATE also outperforms DT in 9/9 tasks. 1190
