## VideoLatent: Video-Language Learning via Latent Self-Forcing

Abstract 

Recent advancements in chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning have shown promise in en-hancing video understanding and reasoning ca-pabilities of multimodal large language mod-els (MLLMs). However, existing CoT-based MLLMs require labor-intensive CoT annota-tions and incur substantial training and infer-ence overhead. While visual latent reason-ing has emerged as a more efficient alterna-tive, existing methods primarily focus on im-age tasks and heavily rely on additional super-vision signals for visual latent generation ( e.g ., CoT traces, auxiliary images, or fine-grained annotations), limiting their scalability and trans-ferability to video tasks. To bridge this gap, we introduce VideoLatent , a novel MLLM equipped with a latent injection module tai-lored for video understanding and reasoning. Specifically, VideoLatent learns to perform vi-sual latent reasoning using a new latent self-forcing training paradigm, which comprises la-tent alignment and latent diversity objectives, and relies solely on standard video-question-answer triplets. Extensive experiments across 14 benchmarks demonstrate that our model consistently outperforms existing standard and latent MLLMs on general video understand-ing and complex video reasoning. Compared with Video-R1, our VideoLatent achieves su-perior computational efficiency, reducing train-ing/inference overhead by ∼6×/∼68 ×. More-over, experiments demonstrate that our method has strong generalizability to different MLLM backbones and different model scales.

2 Related Work 

MLLLMs for Video Understanding and Reason-ing. With the rapid evolution of large language models (LLMs) (Vaswani et al., 2017; Touvron et al., 2023; DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Brown et al., 2020; OpenAI, 2023, 2025; Team, 2023; Reid et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024a; Team, 2025b), multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) have wit-nessed remarkable progress in the vision-language domain (Liu et al., 2023, 2024a; Li et al., 2024a, 2025b; Bai et al., 2023; Li et al., 2026, 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025d; Ye et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2023; Maaz et al., 2024; Deitke et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025d; Liu et al., 2025c; Dai et al., 2023; He et al., 2025; Assran et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025a). For example, existing MLLMs have demonstrated strong performance on general video understanding benchmarks (Zhao et al., 2025; 2Krojer et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024a; Patraucean et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024; Cores et al., 2024; Shangguan et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025c). To further enhance complex video reasoning ability (Hu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025a,b; Chen et al., 2025c,b; Cheng et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025), recent efforts have explored incorporating explicit chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning into MLLMs (Wei et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023, 2024b; Zhong et al., 2024; Mitra et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025d; Tian et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025e). Specifically, Video-R1 (Feng et al., 2025a) pro-poses T-GRPO ( i.e ., a variant of GRPO (Shao et al., 2024)) to efficiently enable R1-style (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) reasoning over videos, while Vide-oRFT (Wang et al., 2025a) strengthens the alignment between textual reasoning and vi-sual evidence via a semantic-consistency reward. VideoChat-R1.5 (Yan et al., 2025) further improves multimodal reasoning by iteratively refining its fo-cus on fine-grained regions. OneThinker (Feng et al., 2025b) optimizes multimodal reasoning by employing a new EMA-GRPO algorithm, whereas Open-o3-Video (Meng et al., 2025) learns to in-tegrate explicit spatio-temporal evidence into the video reasoning process. Concurrently, VideoAuto-R1 (Liu et al., 2026a) introduces a reason-when-necessary strategy to facilitate dynamic CoT rea-soning, while still incurring significant training overhead ( i.e ., 1120 H100 GPU hours). Despite their promising performance, existing CoT-based video reasoning approaches primarily rely on explicit CoT reasoning and additional labor-intensive CoT supervision (Feng et al., 2025a), re-sulting in substantial computational overhead and limited training scalability. In contrast, we pro-pose VideoLatent, which introduces a new latent injection module and latent self-forcing to enhance video-language learning without explicit CoT an-notations, while maintaining strong performance and high computational efficiency. 

Visual Latent Reasoning. Latent reasoning has demonstrated strong effectiveness in NLP, enabling LLMs to perform reasoning beyond the constraints of language (Goyal et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2025b; Cheng and Durme, 2024; Xu et al., 2025c; Zou et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026). For example, (Hao et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025b) learn to switch between language and latent reason-ing modes. Instead of generating explicit textual thoughts, latent reasoning performs implicit rea-soning in continuous latent spaces, enabling more efficient and flexible reasoning. Recently, latent reasoning has been extended to a wide range of do-mains, including image reasoning (Wu et al., 2026; Han et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025a; Shen et al., 2025a; Tong et al., 2025), 3D reasoning (Chen et al., 2025e), image generation (Chen et al., 2026), robotics (Ma et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2026b), and autonomous driving (Luo et al., 2026), with some works using foundation models ( e.g ., Diffu-sion (Ma et al., 2025a; Shao et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025a; He et al., 2024) and DINO (Jeon et al., 2026)) to supervise visual latent generation. In particular, visual latent reasoning has emerged as a promising direction for enhancing the multi-modal understanding and reasoning capabilities of MLLMs (Bigverdi et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2025). For instance, Mirage (Yang et al., 2025c) learns to reconstruct the image embeddings of helper images as visual latent representations, while ILVR (Dong et al., 2025) instead learns to selectively extract context-relevant visual signals from helper images. Furthermore, CoVT (Qin et al., 2025) leverages multiple pretrained vision foundation models (Kir-illov et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024b; Su et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2024) to supervise the visual latent generation. Concurrently, LVR (Li et al., 2025a) learns to generate fine-grained visual la-tent representations by reconstructing the embed-dings of cropped image regions based on bound-ing box annotations, whereas Monet (Wang et al., 2025b) further incorporates diverse fine-grained su-pervision signals ( e.g ., cropping, grounding, high-lighting) into visual latent reasoning. Laser (Wang et al., 2026b) learns to align visual latent repre-sentations with cognitive scanpaths annotated by GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024a), while Mull-Tokens (Ray et al., 2025) enables modality-agnostic latent rea-soning using additional annotations, including both textual CoT traces and subgoal images. Despite recent progress, visual latent reasoning for video tasks remains underexplored and faces significant scalability challenges. Specifically, ex-isting methods primarily rely on additional, image-centric supervision signals for visual latent genera-tion ( e.g ., CoT traces (Shen et al., 2025a), helper images (Yang et al., 2025c), pretrained vision foun-dation models (Qin et al., 2025), fine-grained an-notations (Li et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025b, 2026b)), limiting both training scalability and trans-ferability to video tasks. 3.2 Our Model: VideoLatent 

In this section, we detail the design of our Video-Latent, which comprises a latent injection module and learns to perform visual latent reasoning via our proposed latent self-forcing, as shown in Fig. 3. 

VideoLatent Overview. Our VideoLatent extends standard MLLMs by augmenting the output space to include both discrete text tokens and continuous latent thoughts ( i.e ., the last-layer hidden state ht). Specifically, VideoLatent learns to switch between a language mode and a latent mode, with the former serving as the default, as in standard MLLMs. At a given decoding step, VideoLatent enters the latent mode upon predicting a special token 

<|video_latent_start|> , and performs visual latent reasoning in the subsequent decoding steps. During the latent mode, our VideoLatent directly generates the last-layer hidden state ht as the predicted continuous latent thought, instead of generating a discrete text token yt. 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Settings Implementation Details. In this work, our Vide-oLatent models are built upon Qwen2.5-VL (Bai et al., 2025) and Qwen3-VL (Team, 2025c). Dur-ing model training, we strictly follow (Feng et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025a) to limit the maximum number of input video frames to 16 to ensure training efficiency, where each frame is processed at a max resolution of 128 ×28 ×28 pixels and 128 ×32 ×32 pixels for Qwen2.5-VL-based and Qwen3-VL-based models, respectively. During evaluation, we further follow (Feng et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025a) to double the frame resolution (e.g ., 256 ×28 ×28 pixels and 256 ×32 ×32 pixels for VideoLatent-7B and VideoLatent-8B, respec-tively) to facilitate a fair comparison. More imple-mentation details are provided in the Appendix. 

Evaluation Benchmarks. To ensure a compre-hensive evaluation, our experiments are conducted on 14 benchmarks, covering general video un-derstanding and complex video reasoning. Gen-eral video understanding comprises 3 short-video understanding benchmarks ( i.e ., MVBench (Li et al., 2024b), TempCompass (Liu et al., 2024b), Next-QA (Xiao et al., 2021)) and 4 long-video understanding benchmarks ( i.e ., Video-MME (Fu et al., 2025), LongVideoBench (Wu et al., 2024b), LVBench (Wang et al., 2025c), MLVU (Wang et al., 2025c)), while complex video reasoning includes Video-TT (Zhang et al., 2025c), VCR-Bench (Qi et al., 2025), VideoMathQA (Rasheed et al., 2025), MMVU (Zhao et al., 2025), Video-Holmes (Cheng et al., 2025), Video-MMMU (Hu et al., 2025), LongVideo-Reason (Chen et al., 2025c). 

Comparison Baselines. To facilitate a thorough comparison, we compare our models against both standard and latent MLLMs. Standard MLLMs comprise Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B (Bai et al., 2025), Video-R1-7B (Feng et al., 2025a), TinyLLaVA-Video-R1 (Zhang et al., 2025b), VideoRFT-7B (Wang et al., 2025a), Open-o3-Video-7B (Meng et al., 2025), VideoChat-R1.5 (Yan et al., 2025), Qwen3-VL-8B (Team, 2025c), OneThinker-8B (Feng et al., 2025b), and VideoAuto-R1 (Liu et al., 2026a). Latent MLLMs encompass Monet-7B (Wang et al., 2025b), LVR-7B (Li et al., 2025a), and Mull-Token-7B (Ray et al., 2025). We also include three closed-source models, including GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024a), Gemini-2.5-Pro (Team, 2025a), and Seed1.5-VL (Seed, 2025).

5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present VideoLatent, a novel framework specially designed for visual latent reasoning on video understanding and reasoning. Specifically, VideoLatent involves a new latent injection module and latent self-forcing training paradigm, enabling effective latent reasoning with-out relying on additional supervision signals. Ex-tensive experiments conducted on fourteen video-language benchmarks demonstrate that our Vide-oLatent consistently outperforms existing stan-dard and latent MLLMs across general video un-derstanding and complex video reasoning, while achieving superior efficiency and generalizability. 

Limitations 

We summarize the limitations as follows: (1) De-spite our model achieving strong performance across various general video understanding and complex video reasoning benchmarks, the results may not generalize to all video-language bench-marks; (2) Due to computational constraints, the maximum number of input video frames in our current training is limited to 16. Future work could benefit from leveraging higher-quality train-ing data with an increased number of frames to further enhance video-language learning; (3) Since our method relies on self-generated latent thoughts without additional supervision signals, the gener-ated latent thoughts may still be irrelevant to the video and question context; and (4) Despite its su-perior inference efficiency, similar to prior visual latent reasoning works, our implicit latent reason-ing may provide relatively lower interpretability compared with explicit CoT reasoning.

A Implementation details 

MLLM Backbones. In this work, our VideoLatent-7B, VideoLatent-8B models, and VideoLatent-3B are built upon Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025), Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Team, 2025c), and Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025), respectively. Our main experiments are conducted on VideoLatent-7B (e.g ., Tab. 1, Tab. 2, Tab. 5, Tab. 7, Fig. 1, Fig. 2, and Fig. 4), while we validate the generalizability of our method using VideoLatent-8B models ( i.e ., Tab. 3) and VideoLatent-3B ( i.e ., Tab. 4). 

Training details. As mentioned in the main pa-per, for training, we strictly follow (Feng et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025a) to limit the maximum number of input video frames to 16 to ensure train-ing efficiency. Specifically, the maximum reso-lution of each frame is set to 128 ×28 ×28 pixels and 128 ×32 ×32 pixels for Qwen2.5-VL-based and Qwen3-VL-based models, respectively. We train our models for 2 epochs with a per-device batch size of 1 and gradient accumulation steps of 16 on 4 NVIDIA H800 (80GB) GPUs, re-sulting in a total batch size of 64. During training, the modality projector, the LLM, and the proposed latent injection module are trainable, while the vi-sion encoder remains frozen. To ensure training stability, we use the Adam optimizer with a learn-ing rate of 1e-6, while employing a learning rate of 1e-5 on the newly introduced latent injection module. We further apply a linear decay scheduler with a warmup of 100 steps, a weight decay of 0.01, and a maximum gradient norm of 1.0. For the latent self-forcing training paradigm, we set the maximum number of latent reasoning steps 

Nmax = 6 for our VideoLatent-7B to achieve the best performance (as demonstrated in Tab. 6), while using Nmax = 2 for VideoLatent-3B and VideoLatent-8B to reduce training overhead. The queue size of the memory banks and temperature hyperparameter used for contrastive learning (Eq. 8, Eq. 9, and Eq. 10) are set to K = 64 and τ = 0 .2,respectively. The balancing coefficients in Eq. 12 are set to λcl = 0 .1 and λintra = 0 .01 .

Training time cost. For training, we use with DeepSpeed Zero2 (Rasley et al., 2020) to acceler-ate the training, where VideoLatent-7B ( Nmax =6) takes 17 hours ( i.e ., 68 H800 GPU hours), VideoLatent-8B ( Nmax = 2) takes 14 hours (i.e ., 64 H800 GPU hours), and VideoLatent-3B (Nmax = 2 ) takes 8 hours ( i.e ., 32 H800 GPU hours). 

Evaluation details. During evaluation, we fur-ther follow (Feng et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025a) to double the frame resolution ( e.g ., 256 ×28 ×28 pixels and 256 ×32 ×32 pixels for VideoLatent-7B and VideoLatent-8B, respectively) to facilitate a fair comparison. All experiments are conducted with 1 NVIDIA H800 GPU. 

Instruction prompt. We use the following in-struction prompt to evaluate our VideoLatent: 

Please think with the given video and answer the multiple-choice question with the option’s letter.\nThe final answer MUST BE put in \\boxed{}. When reproduc-ing the experimental results of the baselines, we use their official system and instruction prompts provided in their paper or official code. For prior visual latent reasoning works, we adopt the multi-image setting to evaluate the performance of Monet-7B (Wang et al., 2025b)and LVR-7B (Li et al., 2025a), while Mull-Token-7B directly takes video inputs.
